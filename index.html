PERT Network Analysis

Computing probabilities of project completion

The textbook shows a project with critical path A–B–E–G–I (22 days) and three other paths. When there are two critical paths, use the one with the largest total variance for probability calculations.

"A conservative approach dictates using the critical path with the largest total variance to focus management's attention on the activities most likely to exhibit broad variations." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 99)
PERT Expected Time
\[T_E = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6}\]
Where a = optimistic, m = most likely, b = pessimistic time estimate.
PERT Variance
\[\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{b - a}{6}\right)^2\]
Variance of each activity's time estimate.
Project Completion Probability
\[Z = \frac{D - T_E}{\sqrt{\sum \sigma^2_{cp}}}\]
From textbook: TE = 38 weeks, D = 35 weeks, Z = −0.87, P = 0.1922 (19% chance of completing in 35 weeks).

📈 Ch 5ABreak-Even Analysis

Break-Even Analysis

📚

Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) Analysis

Determining the volume at which total revenue equals total cost

Break-even analysis is a fundamental capacity planning tool that determines the point at which a business neither makes a profit nor incurs a loss. It establishes the relationship between fixed costs, variable costs, selling price, and volume to identify the minimum output needed to cover all costs.

"Break-even analysis is a standard approach to determine the volume of output at which total revenue equals total cost. It is useful for comparing capacity alternatives and for determining the volume needed to achieve a target profit." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 155)

Cost Structure Components

🏢
Fixed Costs (F)
Costs that remain constant regardless of output volume: rent, depreciation, insurance, management salaries.
📦
Variable Costs (V)
Costs that vary directly with output: raw materials, direct labor, packaging, shipping per unit.
💵
Contribution Margin
Price minus Variable Cost (P − V). Each unit sold contributes this amount toward covering fixed costs.
Break-Even Point (Units)
\[BEP_{units} = \frac{F}{P - V}\]
Where F = Total Fixed Costs, P = Selling Price per unit, V = Variable Cost per unit. The denominator (P − V) is the contribution margin per unit.
Break-Even Point (Revenue/Dollars)
\[BEP_{\$} = \frac{F}{1 - \frac{V}{P}} = BEP_{units} \times P\]
Expresses the break-even point in terms of total revenue dollars needed to cover all costs.
Volume for Target Profit
\[Q_{target} = \frac{F + \text{Target Profit}}{P - V}\]
Extends the BEP formula to determine the volume needed to achieve a specific profit goal.
Total Cost & Total Revenue Functions
\[TC = F + V \cdot Q \quad ; \quad TR = P \cdot Q\]
At BEP: TC = TR. Below BEP → Loss; Above BEP → Profit. The profit at any volume Q is: Profit = TR − TC = Q(P − V) − F.
💡 Managerial Insight: Comparing Capacity Alternatives
When comparing two process alternatives (e.g., manual vs. automated), the option with higher fixed costs but lower variable costs will have a higher BEP but becomes more profitable at higher volumes. The indifference point where both options yield equal total cost is: Q* = (F₂ − F₁) / (V₁ − V₂).

Assumptions & Limitations

  • Revenue and costs are linear functions of volume (constant price and variable cost per unit)
  • Fixed costs remain constant within the relevant range of output
  • All units produced are sold (no inventory buildup)
  • Single product analysis (or constant product mix for multi-product)

🌳 Ch 5Decision Trees & Expected Monetary Value

Decision Trees

📚

Expected Monetary Value (EMV) Analysis

Structured decision-making under uncertainty

Decision tree analysis is a quantitative approach for evaluating alternatives that involve sequential decisions and chance events. It provides a visual framework for analyzing decisions under uncertainty by mapping out all possible outcomes, their probabilities, and their monetary consequences.

"A decision tree is a schematic model of alternatives available to the decision maker, along with their possible consequences. The term gets its name from the tree-like appearance of the diagram." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 148)

Decision Tree Components

◻️
Decision Node (□)
A point where the decision maker must choose between alternatives. Represented by a square.
⭕
Chance Node (○)
A point where chance determines the outcome. Probabilities must sum to 1.0. Represented by a circle.
🔺
Terminal Node (△)
The final payoff or consequence at the end of a branch. Represents the monetary outcome.
Expected Monetary Value (EMV)
\[EMV = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (P_i \times V_i)\]
Where Pi = probability of outcome i, Vi = monetary value (payoff) of outcome i, and n = number of possible outcomes. The decision rule: Select the alternative with the highest EMV.
Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI)
\[EVPI = EV_{with\ PI} - EV_{without\ PI}\]
EVPI represents the maximum amount a decision maker should pay for perfect information about future states of nature. It equals the difference between the expected value under certainty and the best EMV without additional information.
💡 Solving Decision Trees: "Roll Back" Method
Decision trees are solved from right to left (backward induction). At each chance node, calculate the EMV. At each decision node, select the alternative with the highest EMV. This process "rolls back" the tree to determine the optimal initial decision.

📉 Ch 6Learning Curves

Learning Curves

📚

Learning Curve Model (Experience Curve)

Predicting cost and time reductions through repetition

The learning curve (also called the experience curve or improvement curve) describes the systematic reduction in production time (or cost) as cumulative output doubles. First documented in the aircraft industry in the 1930s by T.P. Wright, it reflects the phenomenon that workers become more efficient through repetition, process improvements, and organizational learning.

"The learning curve theory is based on three assumptions: (1) the amount of time required to complete a given task or unit of a product will be less each time the task is undertaken, (2) the unit time will decrease at a decreasing rate, and (3) the reduction in time will follow a specific and predictable pattern." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 168)
Unit Time (Log-Linear Model)
\[Y_x = K \cdot x^n\]
Where Yx = time to produce the x-th unit, K = time for the first unit, x = cumulative unit number, and n = learning curve exponent (always negative, indicating improvement).
Learning Curve Exponent
\[n = \frac{\log(b)}{\log(2)}\]
Where b = learning rate (expressed as a decimal, e.g., 0.80 for an 80% learning curve). For an 80% curve: n = log(0.80)/log(2) = −0.322. The negative exponent ensures time decreases as units increase.
Cumulative Average Time
\[\bar{T}_x = K \cdot \frac{x^{n+1} - 0}{x} = K \cdot x^n \quad \text{(approximation)}\]
For precise cumulative calculations, use learning curve tables or the formula: Total time for x units = K · Σ(i^n) for i = 1 to x.
💡 Interpreting the Learning Rate
An "80% learning curve" means that every time cumulative production doubles, the per-unit time drops to 80% of its previous level. Unit 1 = 100 hrs → Unit 2 = 80 hrs → Unit 4 = 64 hrs → Unit 8 = 51.2 hrs. Industries with high manual labor content (e.g., aerospace) typically show 75-85% learning rates.

Applications in Operations Management

  • Cost Estimation: Bidding on contracts by predicting future unit costs
  • Workforce Planning: Determining labor requirements as production ramps up
  • Pricing Strategy: Setting prices based on anticipated cost reductions
  • Make-or-Buy Decisions: Comparing in-house learning benefits vs. outsourcing

📚

Line Balancing Fundamentals

Equalizing work across stations for smooth flow

Assembly line balancing assigns tasks to workstations so that each station has approximately the same amount of work. The goal is to minimize the number of workstations (or maximize efficiency) while respecting precedence constraints and cycle time limits.

Cycle Time
\[C = \frac{\text{Production Time per Day}}{\text{Required Output per Day}}\]
Maximum time allowed at each workstation. Determines the pace of the line.
Theoretical Minimum Workstations
\[N_{min} = \left\lceil \frac{\sum t_i}{C} \right\rceil\]
Where Σti = sum of all task times, C = cycle time. Round up to next integer.
Line Efficiency
\[\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\sum t_i}{N_{actual} \times C} \times 100\%\]
Percentage of productive time. Higher is better. 100% means zero idle time.

👥 Ch 10Queuing Theory (Waiting Line Models)

Queuing Theory

📚

Waiting Line Models

Analyzing the trade-off between service capacity and customer waiting

Queuing theory provides mathematical models to analyze waiting lines (queues). It helps managers balance the cost of providing service capacity against the cost of customers waiting. The fundamental trade-off: more servers reduce waiting but increase costs; fewer servers save money but increase customer dissatisfaction.

"Queuing theory enables us to analyze the relationship between demand on a service system and the delays suffered by users of that system. It helps managers make capacity decisions by quantifying the relationship between arrival rates, service rates, and system performance." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 286)

Kendall's Notation: A/B/s

  • A = Arrival distribution (M = Markovian/Poisson, D = Deterministic, G = General)
  • B = Service time distribution (M = Exponential, D = Deterministic, G = General)
  • s = Number of servers (channels)

Model 1: M/M/1 — Single Server Queue

Assumptions: Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, single server, infinite queue capacity, FIFO discipline, infinite population.

Server Utilization (Traffic Intensity)
\[\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}\]
Where λ = mean arrival rate, μ = mean service rate. System is stable only when ρ < 1 (i.e., λ < μ). Utilization represents the fraction of time the server is busy.
Average Number in System (Ls)
\[L_s = \frac{\lambda}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\]
Total average number of customers in the system (waiting + being served).
Average Number in Queue (Lq)
\[L_q = \frac{\lambda^2}{\mu(\mu - \lambda)} = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}\]
Average number of customers waiting in line (excluding the one being served).
Average Time in System (Ws)
\[W_s = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}\]
Average total time a customer spends in the system (waiting + service).
Average Time in Queue (Wq)
\[W_q = \frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu - \lambda)} = W_s - \frac{1}{\mu}\]
Average time a customer spends waiting in line before service begins.
Probability of Zero in System (P0)
\[P_0 = 1 - \frac{\lambda}{\mu} = 1 - \rho\]
Probability that the system is empty (server is idle).
Probability of n Customers in System
\[P_n = \left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)^n \cdot \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right) = \rho^n(1-\rho)\]
Geometric distribution of system size. Useful for determining the probability of exceeding a certain queue length.

Model 3: M/M/s — Multi-Server Queue

Multiple parallel servers (s channels) drawing from a single queue. Requires λ < sμ for stability.

Probability of Empty System (P0) — Multi-Server
\[P_0 = \left[\sum_{n=0}^{s-1} \frac{(\lambda/\mu)^n}{n!} + \frac{(\lambda/\mu)^s}{s!} \cdot \frac{s\mu}{s\mu - \lambda}\right]^{-1}\]
Foundation for all other multi-server calculations. Must be computed first.
Average Queue Length (Lq) — Multi-Server
\[L_q = \frac{P_0 \cdot (\lambda/\mu)^s \cdot \rho}{s! \cdot (1-\rho)^2}\]
Where ρ = λ/(sμ) for multi-server systems. Other metrics derived via Little's Law: Wq = Lq/λ, Ws = Wq + 1/μ, Ls = λWs.

Model 4: Finite Population (Machine Repair Model)

Used when the calling population is limited (e.g., N machines that can break down).

Average Number Waiting (Finite Population)
\[L_q = N(1 - F)\]
Where N = population size and F = efficiency factor (from finite queuing tables). As machines enter the queue, the effective arrival rate decreases.
💡 Little's Law — The Universal Connector
Little's Law (L = λW) connects all queuing metrics. Once you know any two of {L, λ, W}, you can find the third. This applies to Ls/Ws and Lq/Wq pairs independently: Ls = λWs and Lq = λWq.

🔄 Ch 11Little's Law

Little's Law

📚

Little's Law: I = R × T

The fundamental law of process analysis

Little's Law (proven by John D.C. Little in 1961) is one of the most powerful and general results in operations management. It states that the long-run average number of items in a stable system equals the long-run average arrival rate multiplied by the average time an item spends in the system.

"Little's law states a mathematical relationship between throughput rate, flow time, and the amount of work-in-process inventory. It applies to any stable system, regardless of the distribution of arrivals or service times." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 312)
Little's Law
\[I = R \times T\]
Where I = average inventory (WIP, items in system), R = average throughput rate (flow rate), T = average flow time (time in system). This holds for ANY stable system — manufacturing, services, healthcare, software development.
Rearranged Forms
\[T = \frac{I}{R} \quad ; \quad R = \frac{I}{T}\]
If you know any two variables, you can calculate the third. This makes Little's Law invaluable for process analysis when direct measurement of one variable is difficult.

Applications

  • Manufacturing: If a factory produces 100 units/day (R) and WIP = 500 units (I), then average flow time = 500/100 = 5 days (T)
  • Hospital: If 20 patients arrive/day (R) and average stay = 4 days (T), then average occupancy = 80 patients (I)
  • Call Center: If 60 calls/hour arrive (R) and average call = 5 min (T), then average calls in system = 60 × (5/60) = 5 calls (I)
💡 Reducing Flow Time
Little's Law shows that to reduce flow time (T) without reducing throughput (R), you must reduce WIP inventory (I). This is the theoretical foundation for Lean Manufacturing and JIT systems: reducing WIP directly reduces lead times.

📚

Attribute Control Charts

Monitoring processes using attribute (pass/fail) data

When quality is measured as a proportion (defective/not defective), we use p-charts. When we count the number of defects per unit, we use c-charts. Both use 3-sigma control limits.

p-Chart: Center Line
\[\bar{p} = \frac{\text{Total Defectives}}{\text{Total Inspected}}\]
The overall proportion defective across all samples.
p-Chart: Standard Deviation
\[S_p = \sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}{n}}\]
Where n = sample size. This is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of proportions.
p-Chart: Control Limits
\[UCL = \bar{p} + 3S_p \quad,\quad LCL = \bar{p} - 3S_p\]
If LCL < 0, set LCL = 0. A z-value of 3 gives 99.7% confidence that the process is within these limits.

Process Capability — Cp & Cpk
Measuring process performance relative to specification limits
📚

Process Capability Indices

How well does the process fit within specification limits?

Process capability compares the output of a process to the specification limits. Cp measures potential capability (if centered), while Cpk measures actual capability accounting for any shift from center.

Process Capability Index (Cp)
\[C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\sigma}\]
Measures the spread of the process relative to the specification width. Cp ≥ 1.0 means the process CAN fit within specs if centered.
Actual Process Capability (Cpk)
\[C_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{USL - \bar{X}}{3\sigma},\; \frac{\bar{X} - LSL}{3\sigma}\right)\]
Accounts for process centering. Cpk ≥ 1.33 is typically required. From textbook: process centered at 1.251 with σ = 0.00083 gives Cpk = 1.6.
"Working with our example in Exhibit 13.4, let's assume our process is centered at 1.251 and σ = 0.00083. Cpk = 1.6, which is the smaller number. This is a pretty good capability index because few defects will be produced by this process." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 374)

Centroid Method

Determine the optimal location for a single facility
📚

Weighted Center of Gravity

Minimizing transportation costs based on distance and volume

The Centroid Method (or Center of Gravity method) is a mathematical technique used for finding the optimal location for a single distribution center that will serve multiple destinations. It minimizes the distance-weighted volume of shipments.

"The centroid method considers existing facilities, the distances between them, and the volumes of goods to be shipped."— Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 456)
Centroid Coordinates (X, Y)
\[C_x = \frac{\sum(d_{ix} \times V_i)}{\sum V_i} \quad ; \quad C_y = \frac{\sum(d_{iy} \times V_i)}{\sum V_i}\]
Where \(d_{ix}\) and \(d_{iy}\) are the X and Y coordinates of location \(i\), and \(V_i\) is the volume of goods moved to/from location \(i\).
💡 Key Assumption
This method assumes that transportation costs are directly proportional to distance and volume, and that straight-line (Euclidean) distances accurately represent actual routing.

Aggregate Planning

Balancing supply and demand over the medium term
📚

Sales & Operations Planning (S&OP)

The modern approach to aggregate planning

"Sales and operations planning was coined by companies to refer to the process that helps firms keep demand and supply in balance. In operations management, this process traditionally was called aggregate planning. The new terminology is meant to capture the importance of cross-functional work." — Jacobs & Chase (2024)

Within S&OP, marketing develops a sales plan that extends through the next 3 to 18 months (intermediate-range planning). The operations team then develops an aggregate operations plan to minimize the cost of resources needed to meet demand. Outputs of the plan are planned production rates, aggregate labor requirements, and expected finished good levels.

Pure Planning Strategies

A pure strategy is a simple strategy that uses just one option for meeting demand.

🏃
Chase Strategy
Match the production rate to the order rate by hiring and laying off employees as the order rate varies. Inventory remains low, but workforce instability is high.
📏
Level Strategy
Maintain a stable workforce working at a constant output rate. Shortages and surpluses are absorbed by fluctuating inventory levels.
🔀
Mixed Strategy
A combination of Chase and Level, often utilizing overtime, subcontracting, or part-time workers to handle peaks.
Chase Strategy Cost Function
\[TC_{chase} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} (P_t \times C_r) + \sum_{t=1}^{n} (|W_t - W_{t-1}| \times C_{hf})\]
Where \(P_t\) is production in period t, \(C_r\) is regular cost, \(W_t\) is workforce size, and \(C_{hf}\) is the cost to hire/fire a worker.
Level Strategy Cost Function
\[TC_{level} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} (\bar{P} \times C_r) + \sum_{t=1}^{n} (I_t \times C_h)\]
Where \(\bar{P}\) is average constant production, \(I_t\) is inventory at end of period t, and \(C_h\) is holding cost per unit.

📦 Ch 20Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

EOQ Model

📚

Economic Order Quantity Model

Minimizing total inventory costs through optimal order sizing

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model, developed by Ford W. Harris in 1913, is the most fundamental inventory model. It determines the optimal order quantity that minimizes the sum of annual ordering costs and annual holding costs. The model balances the trade-off: ordering frequently (small Q) reduces holding costs but increases ordering costs, while ordering infrequently (large Q) reduces ordering costs but increases holding costs.

"The EOQ model attempts to find the order quantity that minimizes total annual holding and ordering costs. At the optimal order quantity Q*, the annual ordering cost exactly equals the annual holding cost." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 598)

Cost Components

📝
Ordering Cost (S)
Fixed cost per order: purchase orders, receiving, inspection. Annual ordering cost = (D/Q) × S.
🏪
Holding Cost (H)
Cost to carry one unit for one year: storage, insurance, obsolescence, capital cost. Annual holding cost = (Q/2) × H.
⚖️
Total Cost Trade-off
At Q*, ordering cost = holding cost. The TC curve is relatively flat near Q*, making EOQ robust to estimation errors.
Total Annual Inventory Cost
\[TC = \underbrace{\frac{D}{Q} \cdot S}_{\text{Annual Ordering Cost}} + \underbrace{\frac{Q}{2} \cdot H}_{\text{Annual Holding Cost}}\]
Where D = annual demand, Q = order quantity, S = ordering cost per order, H = annual holding cost per unit. This excludes the purchase cost of items (DC) which is constant regardless of Q.
Optimal Order Quantity (Q*)
\[Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DS}{H}}\]
Derived by setting dTC/dQ = 0 and solving. At Q*, the annual ordering cost (D/Q*)S equals the annual holding cost (Q*/2)H. This is the fundamental result of the EOQ model.
Minimum Total Cost
\[TC_{min} = \sqrt{2DSH}\]
Substituting Q* back into the TC equation. Since ordering cost = holding cost at optimum, TCmin = 2 × (D/Q*)S = 2 × (Q*/2)H.
Number of Orders per Year & Reorder Interval
\[N = \frac{D}{Q^*} \quad ; \quad T = \frac{Q^*}{D} \times 365 \text{ days}\]
N = number of orders placed per year. T = time between orders (order cycle). These help with operational planning and scheduling.
💡 Robustness of EOQ
The total cost curve is relatively flat around Q*. Even if the actual order quantity deviates by ±25% from Q*, total cost increases by less than 3%. This makes EOQ practical even when demand and cost estimates are imprecise. However, Q* is more sensitive to changes in D and S than to changes in H (due to the square root relationship).

EOQ Assumptions

  • Demand is known, constant, and continuous throughout the year
  • Lead time is constant and known
  • No quantity discounts are available
  • Ordering/setup cost is constant per order
  • No stockouts are permitted (all demand is met)
  • The entire order is received at once (instantaneous replenishment)

Safety Stock

📚

Safety Stock & Reorder Point Analysis

Protecting against stockouts due to demand and lead time variability

Safety stock is extra inventory held to buffer against uncertainty in demand and/or lead time. The reorder point (ROP) is the inventory level at which a new order should be placed to avoid stockouts during the lead time period.

Safety Stock (Demand Variability Only)
\[SS = z \cdot \sigma_d \cdot \sqrt{LT}\]
Where z = number of standard deviations for desired service level (from Z-table), σd = standard deviation of daily demand, LT = lead time in days. The √LT factor converts daily variability to lead-time variability.
Safety Stock (Both Demand & Lead Time Variability)
\[SS = z \cdot \sqrt{LT \cdot \sigma_d^2 + \bar{d}^2 \cdot \sigma_{LT}^2}\]
More comprehensive formula accounting for variability in both demand (σd) and lead time (σLT). Use when lead time is also uncertain.
Reorder Point (ROP)
\[ROP = \bar{d} \times LT + SS\]
Where d̄ = average daily demand, LT = lead time in days, SS = safety stock. The first term covers expected demand during lead time; SS covers the uncertainty.

📅 Ch 22Job Sequencing & Priority Rules

Job Scheduling

📚

Priority Sequencing Rules

Determining the order in which jobs are processed

Job sequencing determines the order in which waiting jobs are processed at a work center. Different priority rules optimize different performance criteria — no single rule is best for all objectives simultaneously.

"Priority rules are simple heuristics used to select the order in which jobs will be processed. They are especially important in job shop environments where different jobs compete for the same resources." — Jacobs & Chase (2024, p. 672)

Common Priority Rules

1️⃣
FCFS
First Come, First Served. Process in order of arrival. Fair but often poor on other metrics.
⏱️
SPT
Shortest Processing Time first. Minimizes average flow time, average WIP, and average lateness. Most widely recommended.
📅
EDD
Earliest Due Date first. Minimizes maximum tardiness (lateness). Best for meeting deadlines.
📊
CR
Critical Ratio. Dynamic rule that prioritizes jobs with the smallest ratio of time remaining to work remaining.
Critical Ratio (CR)
\[CR = \frac{\text{Due Date} - \text{Today's Date}}{\text{Processing Time Remaining}}\]
CR < 1: Job is behind schedule (urgent). CR=1: Job is exactly on schedule. CR> 1: Job is ahead of schedule. Process the job with the smallest CR first.

Performance Metrics

Average Flow Time
\[\text{Avg Flow Time} = \frac{\sum \text{Flow Time}_i}{n} = \frac{\sum \text{Completion Time}_i}{n}\]
Average time jobs spend in the system (from arrival to completion). SPT minimizes this metric.
Average Tardiness (Lateness)
\[\text{Avg Tardiness} = \frac{\sum \max(0,\ C_i - D_i)}{n}\]
Where Ci = completion time of job i, Di = due date of job i. Only counts positive lateness (jobs completed after their due date).
Average Number of Jobs in System
\[\text{Avg Jobs in System} = \frac{\sum \text{Flow Time}_i}{\text{Total Processing Time (Makespan)}}\]
Derived from Little's Law. Represents the average WIP level at the work center.
💡 SPT Optimality
SPT is provably optimal for minimizing: (1) average flow time, (2) average number of jobs in the system, and (3) average lateness. However, it can cause long jobs to wait excessively. In practice, a "truncated SPT" rule is often used where jobs exceeding a maximum wait time are promoted.

Critical Ratio

Finding the service level target

The Newsvendor Model (or single-period model) is used when items are perishable or have a limited selling season. The goal is to balance the cost of understocking (lost profit) against the cost of overstocking (unsold items).

\[C_u = \text{Price} - \text{Cost} \quad | \quad C_o = \text{Cost} - \text{Salvage}\]

\[P \leq \frac{C_u}{C_u + C_o}\]

\[Q^* = \mu + z\sigma\]

Probability Distributions for Queuing

The mathematical foundation of waiting line models

Queuing models rely on two key probability distributions. The exponential distribution models the time between arrivals (inter-arrival time) and service times. The Poisson distribution models the number of arrivals in a given time period.

Exponential Distribution (Equation 10.1)
\[f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\]
Probability density function for inter-arrival times. P(service time ≤ t) = 1 − e−λt. The mean of the exponential distribution is 1/λ.
Probability of Completion Within Time t
\[P(t \leq T) = 1 - e^{-\lambda T}\]
Probability that an event occurs within T time units. Complement: P(more than T) = e−λT.
Poisson Distribution (Equation 10.2)
\[P_T(n) = \frac{(\lambda T)^n \cdot e^{-\lambda T}}{n!}\]
Probability of exactly n arrivals in time period T, where λ = mean arrival rate per time unit.
💡 Relationship Between Distributions
If arrivals follow a Poisson process with rate λ, then the time between consecutive arrivals follows an exponential distribution with parameter λ. Mean inter-arrival time = 1/λ. These are two sides of the same coin.

Customer Order Decoupling

Where inventory is positioned in the supply chain

The customer order decoupling point is where inventory is positioned to allow processes or entities in the supply chain to operate independently. Upstream = forecast-driven; downstream = customer-order-driven.

Total Product Configurations (Equation 7.1)
\[\text{Configurations} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} N_i = N_1 \times N_2 \times \cdots \times N_n\]
Example: 3 processors × 3 memory × 4 SSD × 2 LCD × 4 colors = 288 configurations from only 16 components.
📦
Make-to-Stock (MTS)
Produce to forecast, sell from inventory. Fast delivery but high inventory risk. Example: consumer goods.
🔧
Assemble-to-Order (ATO)
Build from pre-made modules when order received. Balance of speed and customization. Example: Dell computers.
📐
Make-to-Order (MTO)
Manufacture after order received. Longer lead time but lower inventory. Example: Boeing aircraft.
✏️
Engineer-to-Order (ETO)
Design and build from scratch. Longest lead time. Example: Lockheed Martin satellites.

Q5
Washers have mean thickness 2.0mm, σ=0.2mm. What fraction is expected to be greater than 2.4mm?
Z = (2.4 − 2.0)/0.2 = 2.0. P(Z > 2.0) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228 or 2.28%

Understanding PERT Estimates

PERT uses three time estimates: Optimistic (a) = best case, Most Likely (m) = normal, Pessimistic (b) = worst case.

\[T_E = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6}\]
2

Calculating Variance

Variance measures uncertainty: σ² = [(b−a)/6]². Higher variance = more risk.

3

Probability Analysis

Using the normal distribution: 68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ of TE.
